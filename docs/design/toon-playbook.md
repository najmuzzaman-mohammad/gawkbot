# Toon Playbook: rubber-hose cartoons, Cuphead, and a design system for cartoon bots

Research compiled 2026-10-09 for a Mac/iOS/web product whose bots are cartoon characters. Part I is the research (history, drawing, animation, sound, Cuphead, character variety, IP). Part II is the playbook: opinionated rules derived from Part I.

Confidence markers used throughout: **[verified]** = stated by a primary or reputable secondary source linked inline; **[reported]** = secondary source, fan wiki, or blog that has not been checked against a primary; **[uncertain]** = sources disagree or the claim is my inference. Nothing here is legal advice.

---

## Part I: Research

### 1. History: the studios and their house styles

"Rubber hose" is the name later given to the dominant American cartoon style of roughly 1920 to 1935: limbs drawn as boneless tubes, round masses, constant motion, and strict synchronisation to the music track. Sources credit Bill Nolan with refining the look while redesigning Felix the Cat in the 1920s, and by the mid-1930s Fleischer, Warner Bros., Walter Lantz, Van Beuren, Terrytoons, Iwerks and Disney each had a version of it ([Laughing Squid](https://laughingsquid.com/rubber-hose-animation/), [Displate](https://blog.displate.com/rubber-hose-animation/)). Disney's *Snow White* (1937) is usually treated as the turning point toward naturalistic "West Coast" animation; Fleischer kept the older look longest, moving to the West Coast style only around 1940 ([Wikipedia: Rubber hose animation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rubber_hose_animation)). **[verified]**

#### Fleischer Studios (New York; Max and Dave Fleischer)

- 1915–1917: Max Fleischer files (1915) and is granted (9 October 1917) the rotoscope patent, a device for tracing live-action film frame by frame ([Fleischer Studios](https://www.fleischerstudios.com/koko.html)). **[verified]**
- 1918/1919: *Out of the Inkwell* begins as a segment in the Bray Pictograph screen magazine; the clown traced from Dave Fleischer's rooftop footage is named Ko-Ko only in 1923 ([Fleischer Studios](https://www.fleischerstudios.com/koko.html)). Start year is given as 1918 by some sources and 1919 by others. **[uncertain on year]**
- September 1924: the "Bouncing Ball" sing-along device debuts in the *Ko-Ko Song Car-Tunes* ([Fleischer Studios](https://www.fleischerstudios.com/koko.html)). 1926: sound-on-film with Lee DeForest, *My Old Kentucky Home* ([Fleischer Studios](https://www.fleischerstudios.com/koko.html)). **[verified]**
- 1929–1932: *Talkartoons*, 42 shorts for Paramount, starring the dog Bimbo ([TV Tropes: Talkartoons](https://www.tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/WesternAnimation/Talkartoons)). Betty Boop first appears on 9 August 1930 in *Dizzy Dishes*, drawn by Grim Natwick as a dog with long ears ([Wikipedia: Betty Boop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Betty_Boop), [Dizzy Dishes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dizzy_Dishes)). By 1932 she has her own series; Koko is revived in 1931 as her co-star ([Fleischer Studios](https://www.fleischerstudios.com/koko.html)). **[verified]**
- 1933: Popeye licensed from King Features; Betty cameos in *Popeye the Sailor* (1933) ([Wikipedia: Betty Boop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Betty_Boop)). **[verified]**
- 1932–1933: three Cab Calloway cartoons, *Minnie the Moocher* (1932), *Snow-White* (1933), *The Old Man of the Mountain* (1933), with Calloway's dancing rotoscoped onto characters ([Wikipedia: Minnie the Moocher (film)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minnie_the_Moocher_(film)), [PBS American Masters](https://www.pbs.org/wnet/americanmasters/cab-calloway-betty-boop-fleischer-cartoon-history-jazz/19500)). **[verified]**
- 1934: *Poor Cinderella*, the first *Color Classic*, shot in Cinecolor because Disney held three-strip Technicolor exclusively; the next seven Color Classics used two-colour Technicolor (reds and greens) until Disney's exclusivity ended ([Wikipedia: Color Classics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Color_Classics), [Fleischer Studios on Betty's red hair](https://www.fleischerstudios.com/betty5.html)). **[verified]**
- 1933–1936: the "Setback" (often mislabelled "Stereoptical") camera: a miniature forced-perspective set on a turntable behind a vertical glass platen holding the cels, rotated slightly per frame. First patent filed 2 November 1933; used at scale in *Popeye the Sailor Meets Sindbad the Sailor* (1936) on a twelve-foot turntable ([FilmDaft case study](https://filmdaft.com/early-3d-in-animation-popeye-the-sailor-meets-sindbad-the-sailor-case-study/), [Animation Studies blog](https://blog.animationstudies.org/the-fleischer-studios-setback-camera-vs-disney-realism/)). Lambiek dates first use to *For Better or Worser* (1935), while *Poor Cinderella* (1934) is also claimed ([Wikipedia: Poor Cinderella](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poor_Cinderella)). **[uncertain on first use]**

House style: urban, nocturnal, surreal, jazz-driven, "adult traumas" rather than "a child's natural fears" (Leonard Maltin, quoted in [course notes on *Of Mice and Magic*](https://lin-web.clarkson.edu/~fbailey/lf390/9-25.html)); grungy Depression-era settings ([Smithsonian](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/arts-culture/the-evolution-of-betty-boop-180979666/)); motion that "violated physical laws" and a mechanical, evenly timed twitch in step with the score ([MoMA](https://www.moma.org/explore/inside_out/2011/02/08/disney-iwerks-and-fleischer-in-the-1930s/), [TV Tropes](https://www.tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/WesternAnimation/Talkartoons)). Everything has a face and will come alive, especially in *Swing You Sinners!* (1930) and *Bimbo's Initiation* (1931) ([Quickdraw Animation](https://quickdrawanimation.ca/discover/monday-shorts/david-fleischer-swing-you-sinners)). The mid-1930s Paramount title cards were hand-lettered by an unidentified studio calligrapher whose style Jerry Beck calls "as much a part of the studio's style as the animation" ([Cartoon Brew](https://www.cartoonbrew.com/classic/fleischerfamous-lettering-3397.html)). **[verified]**

#### Disney (Los Angeles)

- 1928: *Steamboat Willie* (Mickey's sound debut); 1929: *The Opry House*, where Mickey first wears gloves ([Creative Bloq](https://creativebloq.com/art/character-design/i-just-learned-why-disney-characters-wear-white-gloves-and-my-childhood-is-ruined)). **[verified]**
- 1929–1939: 75 *Silly Symphonies*, conceived by Carl Stalling as music-first shorts with no recurring star ([Wikipedia: Silly Symphony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silly_Symphony)); *The Skeleton Dance* (1929) is the first ([Wikipedia: Carl W. Stalling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_W._Stalling)). **[verified]**
- 30 July 1932: *Flowers and Trees*, first commercial three-strip Technicolor film; Disney then signed an exclusive on the three-strip process for animation (two or three years depending on source; commonly "until the end of 1935", a claim Wikipedia flags as needing citation), forcing Iwerks and Fleischer onto two-colour systems ([Wikipedia: Flowers and Trees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flowers_and_Trees)). **[verified, end date uncertain]**
- 27 May 1933: *Three Little Pigs*; 1935: *The Band Concert*, the first colour Mickey ([Wikipedia: The Band Concert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Band_Concert)). **[verified]**

House style: pastoral, "wholesome" Midwestern values ([MoMA](https://www.moma.org/explore/inside_out/2011/02/08/disney-iwerks-and-fleischer-in-the-1930s/)), musical, increasingly naturalistic, with the studio's experiments in effects, camera and colour run through the Silly Symphonies ([Wikipedia: Silly Symphony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silly_Symphony)). Production: drawings traced in India ink onto 5/1000-inch celluloid, painted on the reverse ([D23](https://d23.com/at-the-helm-of-ink-paint/), [1933 *International Projectionist*](https://www.dix-project.net/document/international-projectionist_1933-05_production-process-for-animated-cartoons)); early backgrounds were light ink and watercolour washes over pencil ([Animation Art Conservation](https://animationartconservation.com/a-blade-of-grass.html)). **[verified]**

#### Ub Iwerks Studio (1930–1936)

Iwerks left Disney in early 1930 under contract to distributor Pat Powers. *Flip the Frog* debuted in *Fiddlesticks* (16 August 1930), the first colour sound cartoon; series ran to 1933. *Willie Whopper* ran 1933–34 (14 shorts). The *ComiColor* fairy-tale series (1933–36) was shot in Cinecolor. The studio closed in 1936 ([Wikipedia: Iwerks Studio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iwerks_Studio), [Flip the Frog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flip_the_Frog), [ComiColor Cartoons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ComiColor_Cartoons)). House style: Disney-adjacent drawing (Iwerks animated *Steamboat Willie*) with a weaker story sense and a red-orange/green-blue two-colour palette. **[verified]**

#### Harman-Ising (Warner Bros., then Van Beuren, then MGM)

Hugh Harman and Rudolf Ising made Bosko for Leon Schlesinger; *Sinkin' in the Bathtub* (April/May 1930) launched *Looney Tunes* (the title a parody of *Silly Symphonies*); *Merrie Melodies* followed in 1931 as one-shot musicals. They left Schlesinger over budgets in 1933 and made two Cubby Bear shorts for Van Beuren before going to MGM ([Wikipedia: Harman and Ising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harman_and_Ising)). House style: Disney imitators with song-plugging structure (Warner owned music publishers). **[verified]**

#### Van Beuren (New York; 1928–1936)

Inherited Paul Terry's *Aesop's Fables* after Terry was fired in 1929; the John Foster era produced "some of the strangest American animated cartoons of the early sound era" ([Intanibase review](https://intanibase.com/gac/reviews/2005/aesop)). *Tom and Jerry* (a tall/short human duo, not the cat and mouse) ran 1931–33 from *Wot a Night* (1 August 1931) ([Wikipedia: Tom and Jerry (Van Beuren)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tom_and_Jerry_(Van_Beuren))). The colour *Rainbow Parade* (1934–36, Molly Moo-Cow) under Burt Gillett is its most polished work ([Animated Views](https://animatedviews.com/2011/aesops-fables-volumes-1-2/)). House style: story-light, chase-and-dance shorts, crude but inventive. **[verified]**

#### Terrytoons (New Rochelle; founded 1929)

Paul Terry's Farmer Al Falfa dates from 1915/16; Terrytoons spun off Kiko the Kangaroo and Puddy the Pup from stock designs. Terry "was far less interested in creating art than he was in providing commerce" ([Wikipedia: Terrytoons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terrytoons)). House style: cheapest, most formulaic of the group; useful mainly as a catalogue of generic barnyard animals. **[verified]**

Walter Lantz (Universal) also produced rubber-hose Oswald shorts in 1930 (*Henpecked*, *Broadway Folly*, *Spooks*) that The Cuphead Show's creators studied ([Cartoon Brew](https://www.cartoonbrew.com/cartoon-study/the-rubber-hose-classics-that-inspired-the-cuphead-show-213700.html)). **[verified]**

### 2. The drawing style

- **Rubber-hose limbs.** Arms and legs are tubes of constant width with no elbow or knee; this gave "smoother movement with less effort" under early technical limits ([Rebusfarm](https://rebusfarm.net/blog/the-rubber-hose-art-style-that-defined-animation)). **[verified]**
- **Pie-cut eyes.** Solid black pupils with a wedge sliced out, common through the 1920s and 1930s and fading when colour became standard in the early 1940s; Betty Boop's cast, Felix in some shorts, and early Mickey all used them ([Tropedia](https://tropedia.fandom.com/wiki/Pie-Eyed), [Adobe](https://www.adobe.com/uk/creativecloud/animation/discover/rubber-hose-animation.html)). I found no primary source explaining the wedge; the common explanations (a drawn-in highlight, a gaze cue, ink-era economy) are **[uncertain]**.
- **White gloves, and why.** Black hands vanished against black bodies in black-and-white film, so gloves gave contrast; they also needed less drawing. Walt Disney: "Five fingers looked like too much on such a little figure, so we took one away." The gloves also carry the minstrel-show costume lineage that historian Nicholas Sammond documents ([Hypebeast](https://hypebeast.com/2017/2/history-of-cartoon-gloves), [Open Culture](https://www.openculture.com/2017/06/why-cartoon-characters-wear-gloves-a-curious-trip-through-the-history-of-animation.html), [/Film](https://slashfilm.com/1733600/why-cartoon-characters-wear-gloves)). **[verified]**
- **Bulbous shoes, black body masses, white faces.** Same contrast logic: a black silhouette reads on a white background; feet are big simple ovals that plant clearly. This is the inkblot-era silhouette convention described across the sources above. **[verified by description, no single primary]**
- **Anthropomorphic everything.** Houses, barns, moons, flowers, cups and kitchen gear all get faces and dance; *Swing You Sinners!* is the canonical example ([Quickdraw Animation](https://quickdrawanimation.ca/discover/monday-shorts/david-fleischer-swing-you-sinners)). **[verified]**
- **Line and paint.** Pencil animation drawings were traced in India ink onto cels and painted from the back in opaque paint ([1933 *International Projectionist*](https://www.dix-project.net/document/international-projectionist_1933-05_production-process-for-animated-cartoons)). The visible "boil" of a hand-traced line is a by-product of every frame being a separate tracing, which Cuphead deliberately preserved as "human imperfections" ([Wikipedia: Cuphead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuphead)). Backgrounds were washes (ink, watercolour) over pencil ([Animation Art Conservation](https://animationartconservation.com/a-blade-of-grass.html)); gouache is frequently claimed but I found no primary confirming it. **[gouache uncertain]**
- **Colour.** Black-and-white era: characters are black masses with white faces/gloves and a few grey tones; backgrounds grey wash. Two-colour era (Cinecolor, two-colour Technicolor): red-orange and green-blue records only; no true blues, purples or yellows ([SCMP review of *The Dawn of Technicolor*](https://amp.scmp.com/lifestyle/books/article/1730801/book-review-dawn-technicolor-1915-1935-birth-colour-movies), [Wikipedia: Cinecolor](https://Www.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cinecolor)). Three-strip Technicolor (Disney from 1932, others from 1935/36) gave the full saturated poster palette ([Wikipedia: Flowers and Trees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flowers_and_Trees)). **[verified]**
- **Typography and title cards.** Main titles were hand-lettered; Fleischer/Paramount's calligrapher produced a bouncy, chunky, slightly irregular display lettering later revived commercially by Mark Simonson as Snicker and Kinescope ([Cartoon Brew](https://www.cartoonbrew.com/classic/fleischerfamous-lettering-3397.html)). Betty's name grew on the Talkartoons cards until it crowded out Bimbo and Koko ([Cartoon Research](https://cartoonresearch.com/index.php/the-end-of-the-talkartoons/)). Live-action titles of the period used Art Deco geometry (*King Kong*, 1933) and genre-coded lettering: haphazard for slapstick, poster type for Westerns ([Canva film titles](https://www.canva.com/learn/film-titles/), [Linearity](https://www.linearity.io/blog/movie-title-design/)). **[verified]**

### 3. Animation

- **Frame rate and ones/twos.** Sound film runs at 24 fps. The Fleischer-era studios drew many scenes on ones (24 drawings/s) before halving to twos (12 drawings/s) for cost; Cuphead's Maja Moldenhauer describes this and says Cuphead is "on the ones" ([Inverse](https://www.inverse.com/article/36647-xbox-one-cuphead-animation), [Wikipedia: Inbetweening](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inbetweening)). **[verified]**
- **The twelve principles** (squash and stretch, anticipation, staging, straight-ahead vs pose-to-pose, follow-through and overlapping action, slow in/out, arcs, secondary action, timing, exaggeration, solid drawing, appeal) were codified by Frank Thomas and Ollie Johnston in *The Illusion of Life* (1981) from 1930s Disney practice ([summary](https://www.simonwhatley.co.uk/writing/disney-12-basic-principles-of-animation/)). **[verified]**
- **Bar sheets and the beat.** Directors timed scenes on bar sheets in beats; 12 frames per beat = 120 BPM, 8 frames = 180 BPM, 16 frames = 90 BPM at 24 fps ([GlobalSpec: Timing on bar sheets](https://www.globalspec.com/reference/39922/203279/timing-on-bar-sheets), [IADT paper](https://archive.onshow.iadt.ie/wp-content/uploads/sites/6/2022/04/A-Brief-History-of-Sound-and-Musical-Timing-in-Animation-Production-Research-Paper-David-Shepstone-N00213825.pdf)). The beat falls at the bottom of the bouncing ball's arc, like a conductor's downbeat ([Cartoon Research: the Rufle Baton](https://cartoonresearch.com/index.php/presenting-the-rufle-baton/)). **[verified]**
- **The "Fleischer bob".** Early sound cartoons had the music recorded first and the animation fitted to it, so characters bob or sway on every beat even when idle ([Straight Dope thread](https://boards.straightdope.com/t/why-do-the-characters-in-very-old-animated-cartoons-constantly-bob-up-down/143121), [TV Tropes](https://www.tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/WesternAnimation/Talkartoons)). The name "Fleischer bob" is informal; no primary source uses it. **[reported]**
- **Walk cycles.** 12 frames per step (two steps a second) reads as a natural walk; 8 is a fast walk or run; 16 is a slow or cartoon walk; 24+ is leisurely; older 2D favoured 8s and 16s because they divide cleanly on twos ([Monmouth University notes on Williams](https://animation.monmouth.edu/instruct/?p=351), [AnimSchool](https://blog.animschool.edu/?p=588), [Brian Lemay](https://brianlemay.com/Pages/animationschool/animation/1stsemesteranimation/walks/walkcycle1a.html)). **[verified]**
- **Takes.** A basic take is start, anticipation, accent, settle: roughly 4 frames easing into the anticipation and 7 into the accent; the Warner-style take holds the anticipation about 6 frames and pops to the accent with no inbetweens ([School of Motion](https://www.schoolofmotion.com/blog/how-to-animate-character-takes)). A double take adds two head shakes before the up ([Lemay](https://brianlemay.com/Pages/animationschool/animation/2ndsemesteranimation/05assignment.html)). **[verified]**
- **Smears and multiples.** Smears (one or two distorted inbetweens, "elongated inbetweens") existed sparingly in the 1930s, with drybrush streaks in Disney's *The Tortoise and the Hare* (1935); Chuck Jones's *The Dover Boys* (10 September 1942) popularised them with more than three dozen. Multiples (drawing several copies of a limb along the arc) are associated mostly with Warner ([Wikipedia: Smear frame](https://Www.Wikipedia.org/wiki/Smear_frame), [Cartoon Research](https://cartoonresearch.com/index.php/the-partial-and-mostly-lost-history-of-animation-smear-terminology/), [Wikipedia: Dover Boys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Dover_Boys_at_Pimento_University)). **[verified]**
- **Rotoscoping.** Fleischer's rotoscope traced live footage; used for Koko (1918) and for Calloway's dances in 1932–33 ([Laughing Squid](https://laughingsquid.com/how-rotoscope-cab-calloway-changed-animation/)). **[verified]**
- **Loops.** 1930s cartoons reused cycles for budget; Cuphead's Chad Moldenhauer cites this as the reason Cuphead shows its idle loops openly rather than hiding them with transitions ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/art/animating-i-cuphead-i-the-verve-of-the-1930s-with-the-tech-of-now)). **[verified]**

### 4. Sound and music

- **Mickey Mousing.** Carl Stalling composed and arranged *The Skeleton Dance* (1929); the Disney practice of fitting music to on-screen action became known as "Mickey Mousing". Disney's "tick system" (a punched film loop clicking on the sound head, credited to sound-effects man Jimmy MacDonald) was a forerunner of the click track ([Wikipedia: Carl W. Stalling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_W._Stalling), [Wikipedia: The Haunted House (1929)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Haunted_House_(1929_film))). Stalling left Disney in 1930 for Iwerks, then followed Iwerks to Schlesinger/Warner in 1936, where he scored nearly every short until retirement using Warner's fifty-piece orchestra and song library ([Wikipedia: Carl W. Stalling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_W._Stalling)). **[verified]**
- **Effects.** Warner's Treg Brown (1936–63) built the effects library from field recordings, a garage of noisemakers and raids on feature-film sound ([Wikipedia: Treg Brown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treg_Brown)). The pit-orchestra instruments as effects (slide whistle, xylophone, muted trumpet "wah", ratchet, cowbell, temple blocks) are standard cartoon-sound lore; I found no 1930s primary listing them, so treat the specific instrument-to-gag mapping in Part II as practice, not history. **[reported]**
- **Jazz.** The Fleischers used jazz, and Cab Calloway specifically, to distinguish themselves from Disney; the studio was near the Harlem clubs ([PBS American Masters](https://www.pbs.org/wnet/americanmasters/cab-calloway-betty-boop-fleischer-cartoon-history-jazz/19500)). **[verified]**
- **Optical soundtracks.** Variable-density and variable-area tracks both exposed the film strip; the Academy mono standard (circa 1938) was variable-area. The "Academy curve" (1938) was mapped to the worst theatres of the day: roll-off beginning around 1–2 kHz and reaching −20 dB or more by 8 kHz, with inherent hiss and narrow dynamic range ([Home Theater HiFi](https://hometheaterhifi.com/volume_9_2/feature-article-curves-6-2002.html), [Cinema Canada 1976](https://cinemacanada.athabascau.ca/index.php/cinema/article/download/535/607.pdf), [pspatialaudio](https://pspatialaudio.com/film_sound.htm)). **[verified; exact hiss dB unknown]**
- **Cuphead's score.** Kristofer Maddigan (a National Ballet of Canada percussionist and childhood friend of the Moldenhauers) wrote 51 tracks, nearly three hours, for a 13-piece big band, a 10-piece ragtime ensemble, solo piano, a vocalist, a tap dancer and the barbershop quartet Shoptimus Prime; recorded live at Canterbury Music Company, Toronto, engineered by Jeremy Darby, contracted and conducted by John Herberman, with many players from Rob McConnell's Boss Brass. Everything was live except a theremin part and some MIDI in "Mausoleum" and "Coin-Op Bop". Chad Moldenhauer pushed for a 1930s big-band direction rather than the chamber-orchestral sound of most actual Fleischer shorts ([VGMOnline interview](https://vgmonline.net/kristofer-maddigan-interview/), [VGMdb](https://vgmdb.net/album/71570), [Cuphead wiki OST](https://cuphead.wiki.gg/wiki/Cuphead_Original_Soundtrack), [Inverse](https://www.inverse.com/article/36767-cuphead-xbox-one-music-kris-maddigan), [Bandcamp Daily](https://daily.bandcamp.com/cuphead-soundtrack-interview)). Tracks have real endings, not fades; music is not synced to game events. "Die House" (King Dice) references "Minnie the Moocher" and is sung by Alana Bridgewater ([Wikipedia: King Dice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Dice)). The DLC score used an ensemble of over 110 performers ([Nintendo](https://www.nintendo.com/whatsnew/behind-the-scenes-music-details-are-best-served-by-the-devs-themselves)). The soundtrack topped Billboard's Jazz Albums chart in September 2019 ([Wikipedia: Cuphead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuphead)). **[verified]**
- **Cuphead's effects.** Sweet Justice (Samuel Justice, Chris Sweetman) built most sounds from scratch: Elder Kettle's voice is a person speaking into a large pot while rattling the lid; the level-complete rise is porcelain cups tapped with small drumsticks; Cala Maria's laugh was recorded with the performer's head in a bowl of water; the genie's laugh is modulated guitar feedback. The vintage feel came from an offline chain of about 10 plugins (tape ADT, a 1930s-style microphone emulation, modelled EQs, reverb to push sources "a few feet from the mic") plus a runtime mastering bus with compression, EQ, pitch/frequency manipulation, modulated flanging and sidechain-style distortion so effects and music interact "as though we'd just pulled out a stream of old optical tape". Effects were band-limited, only about 30% of created sounds shipped, and effects were restricted to gameplay-critical cues so the music stayed primary ([A Sound Effect interview](https://www.asoundeffect.com/cuphead-sound/)). **[verified]**

### 5. Cuphead's creation

- **Timeline.** Chad and Jared Moldenhauer attempted a similar game around 2000; development proper began in 2010 in XNA after *Super Meat Boy*; announced 2013 (teaser 25 October 2013); shown at Microsoft's E3 2014 press event at about 40% complete and moved to Unity that year; shown again at E3 2015; the brothers remortgaged their homes in 2015; a 2016 date slipped to 29 September 2017 (Windows, Xbox One). Ports: macOS 19 October 2018, Switch 18 April 2019, PS4 28 July 2020 ([Wikipedia: Cuphead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuphead), [Cuphead wiki](https://cuphead.wiki.gg/wiki/Cuphead_(game)), [Unity case study](https://unity.com/madewith/cuphead), [GameSpot](https://www.gamespot.com/articles/how-cupheads-devs-gambled-on-a-dream/1100-6454860/)). Sales: over a million in two weeks (the article lead says two million), six million by July 2020 ([Wikipedia: Cuphead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuphead)). **[verified; two-week figure conflicts]**
- **Team.** Chad Moldenhauer (co-director, art: drew animation and painted backgrounds), Jared Moldenhauer (co-director, design), Marija "Maja" Moldenhauer (producer and inker), Tony Coculuzzi (programming lead), Jake Clark (animator; GDC 2017 talk on boss animation), Hanna Abi-Hanna (2018 Annie for character animation), Tina Nawrocki (Annie nominee), Evan Skolnick (writer) ([Wikipedia: Cuphead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuphead), [Game Anim](https://www.gameanim.com/2017/08/18/cupheads-animation-process-philosophy/)). **[verified]**
- **Pipeline.** Everything pencilled and inked on paper, then coloured in Photoshop; a cel test showed digital colouring was indistinguishable, so cels were dropped. Backgrounds hand-painted in watercolour. Animation on ones at 24 fps while gameplay runs at 60 fps; over 120,000 hand-drawn frames. The team found only five North American artists still animating this way and taught themselves from books and YouTube ([Unity](https://unity.com/madewith/cuphead), [Inverse](https://www.inverse.com/article/36647-xbox-one-cuphead-animation), [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/art/animating-i-cuphead-i-the-verve-of-the-1930s-with-the-tech-of-now), [GameSpot](https://www.gamespot.com/articles/how-cupheads-devs-gambled-on-a-dream/1100-6454860/)). Maja Moldenhauer on why paper: digital tools could not capture "the taper of a pen stroke" or shifting line weight ([Inverse](https://www.inverse.com/article/36647-xbox-one-cuphead-animation)). **[verified]**
- **Influences.** Chad calls Fleischer the "magnetic north" of the style; also Disney, Warner, MGM, Lantz, and animators Ub Iwerks, Grim Natwick and Willard Bowsky. Cuphead's head descends from a 1936 Japanese propaganda cartoon ([Wikipedia: Cuphead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuphead)). *Swing You Sinners!* (1930) is the single most-cited short ([Inverse](https://www.inverse.com/article/36647-xbox-one-cuphead-animation)). **[verified]**
- **Film look.** The game ships a Film Grain toggle and a Color Filter (sepia, desaturated) in the video options, and a black-and-white filter plus "vintage audio mode" unlock ([Ludo guide](https://www.ludo.guide/guide/cuphead/technical-performance/graphics-settings-customization), [Magic Game World](https://www.magicgameworld.com/cuphead-how-to-unlock-black-white-filter-mode-vintage-audio-mode/)); accessibility mods remove "film grain, color bleed and blur" ([Steam thread](https://steamcommunity.com/app/268910/discussions/0/1696043263490190101)). Documented inspirations: Cagney Carnation's palms-out dance and part of his music come from a ghost in *Swing You Sinners!*; the Baroness's walking castle echoes that short's marching barn ([TV Tropes: Swing You Sinners](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Trivia/SwingYouSinners), [Bogleech](https://bogleech.com/halloween/hall17-cuphead.html)); King Dice is a tribute to Cab Calloway ([Wikipedia: King Dice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Dice)); Dr. Kahl and Grim Matchstick are named for Disney's Milt Kahl and Fleischer's Grim Natwick ([WatchMojo](https://www.watchmojo.com/articles/10-things-you-didn-t-know-about-cuphead)). Claims that Wally Warbles echoes a Popeye bird or Woody Woodpecker, and Cala Maria a 1938 moth short, are fan lists. **[reported]** Reviewers note Djimmi carries the Orientalist clichés of the originals ([Hardcore Gaming 101](https://www.hardcoregaming101.net/cuphead/)), a reminder that the era's caricature conventions include racist ones to be excluded deliberately ([Unwinnable](https://unwinnable.com/2017/11/10/cuphead-and-the-racist-spectre-of-fleischer-animation/)).

How bosses are differentiated: each has a distinct class (above), a distinct silhouette, 3 to 6 phases that transform the body (Hilda has six, the most) ([High Ground Gaming](https://www.highgroundgaming.com/cuphead-bosses-ranked/)), a hand-lettered alliterative title, and a dedicated musical idiom (Latin for "Floral Fury", silent-film for "Forest Follies", Ellington for "Pyramid Peril") ([Bandcamp Daily](https://daily.bandcamp.com/cuphead-soundtrack-interview)). Personality is carried by props and costume rather than facial detail: a top hat and cane for a dandy, a bow tie for a friendly host, a captain's hat and hook, a chef's toque, a clown's ruff, a cigar for a tough; posture (lean-back swagger versus hunched grouch) does the rest. This is the same vocabulary the 1930s used, where a derby and a cigar made a villain and a bow made a sweetheart.

### 7. Intellectual property

- **Style versus expression.** US copyright protects original expression, not ideas, procedures or methods (17 U.S.C. §102(b)); the Copyright Office's list of unprotectable material includes "mere variations of typographic ornamentation, lettering, or coloring" ([Circular 33](https://www.copyright.gov/circs/circ33.pdf)). A visual style (rubber-hose limbs, pie eyes, cream-and-ink palette, film grain) is therefore free to use. A specific character design, name, wordmark, background painting, animation frame or music recording is protectable. **[verified, not legal advice]**
- **Characters can be protected independently of a particular film**, and recognisable designs also function as trademarks that do not expire while in use ([NPR on Steamboat Willie](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/1221606624), [IIPLA](https://iipla.org/news/mickey-mouse-enters-us-public-domain-in-2024-amid-complex-copyright-and-trademark-landscape)).
- **What is public domain in the US now (works, not brands):**
  - 2023: Oswald the Lucky Rabbit's original shorts and 1927 design; Felix the Cat's silent output ([Cartoon Brew](https://www.cartoonbrew.com/books/2023-public-domain-additions-oswald-metropolis-224596.html)).
  - 1 January 2024: *Steamboat Willie* and *Plane Crazy* Mickey (1928). Later Mickey designs remain under copyright; the name and the modern gloved design are registered trademarks ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/1221606624), [/Film](https://slashfilm.com/1482137/mickey-mouse-steamboat-willie-public-domain-disney)).
  - 1 January 2025: *The Skeleton Dance*; Popeye as he appears in the 1929 *Thimble Theatre* strip; Tintin's first appearances (US only; EU until 2054) ([Copyright Lately](https://copyrightlately.com/public-domain-2025/), [KQED](https://kqed.org/arts/13969786/popeye-tintin-enter-public-domain-in-2025)).
  - 1 January 2026: Betty Boop as in *Dizzy Dishes* (1930, dog-eared); Flip the Frog; Pluto's prototype "Rover" from *The Picnic* (named Pluto follows in 2027); 1930 Bimbo and Bosko shorts by the same arithmetic ([Duke CSPD Public Domain Day 2026](https://web.law.duke.edu/cspd/publicdomainday/2026/), [Cartoon Brew](https://www.cartoonbrew.com/classic/public-domain-betty-boop-flip-the-frog-rover-257808.html), [Copyright Lately](https://copyrightlately.com/public-domain-2026/)). **[verified]**
- **Trademarks survive.** *Fleischer Studios v. A.V.E.L.A.* (9th Cir., amended opinion 19 August 2011) shows the mess: Fleischer lost its Betty Boop copyright claim for a broken chain of title, and its image-trademark claim failed for "fractured" ownership history, while the word mark claim was remanded; the withdrawn February 2011 opinion had gone further on aesthetic functionality ([Loeb & Loeb](https://www.loeb.com/en/insights/publications/2011/08/fleischer-studios-inc-v-avela-inc), [Finnegan](https://www.finnegan.com/files/upload/Newsletters/Incontestable/2012/December/Incontestable_Dec12_4.html)). The lesson: a public-domain design can still be a live trademark, and litigating that is expensive even when you win. **[verified]**
- **Cuphead is fully protected.** Everything in the game (2017), DLC (2022) and show (2022) is under copyright, and Studio MDHR's names and marks are trademarks. Fonts extracted from the game files are Studio MDHR assets ([Designbeep](https://designbeep.com/2026/01/14/cuphead-font/)).
- **Rules of thumb for "inspired by, not copied".** (1) Take the grammar (tube limbs, pie eyes, gloves, bob, card-based titles), never a noun (a specific character, name, logo, level title, melody). (2) If a reviewer could name the original character from your silhouette alone, redesign. (3) Do not use public-domain characters as mascots either: the trademark and consumer-confusion risk is the same, and the brand is not yours. (4) Keep a provenance note for each character listing the era references consulted. (5) Never ship the era's racist caricature conventions; the style's grammar does not require them.

## Part II: The playbook

These rules are opinionated. Where a number is stated it is a default to be followed unless a documented reason exists.

### 8. Character design rules

**Proportions.**
- Height 2.5 to 3.5 heads. Head is a circle or a slightly squashed circle; body is a pear or bean 1 to 1.5 heads tall; legs and arms are tubes 0.12 to 0.15 head-diameters wide, constant along their length.
- No elbows, knees, wrists or ankles. A bend is a curve, never a hinge. Hands and feet are the only places a limb changes width.
- Hands: four digits (three fingers and a thumb) in white gloves with a cuff line and two or three stitch marks on the back. Never five fingers; never bare hands on primary characters.
- Feet: oval shoes 0.8 to 1.0 head-widths long with a toe bump; no laces unless the character is a "fussy" type.
- Silhouette test: fill the character black on cream. The class (cup, plant, animal, vehicle) must still read.

**Eyes and face.**
- Pie-cut eyes by default: a black oval pupil with a wedge removed, no iris, no white highlight dot. The wedge points toward the upper-outer corner. Bead eyes (no wedge) only for very small or background characters.
- Face mass is white or cream; body mass may be ink-black (black-and-white era read) or a single saturated colour. Mouth is a single ink line; open mouth is a black shape with a tongue, no teeth unless it is a gag.
- Brows are ink strokes, not shapes, and move independently of the eyes for expression. Expressions are drawn big: a surprised face adds eyes popping out 20 to 40 percent beyond the head outline.

**Props for personality.** Every bot gets exactly one hat, one neckpiece, and one handheld prop from these lists, and nothing else: hat (bowler, top hat, flat cap, chef's toque, captain's cap, straw boater, tiara, nightcap, nothing); neckpiece (bow tie, cravat, bandana, ruff, collar-and-tie, pearls, nothing); prop (cane, monocle, wrench, ladle, pencil, megaphone, clipboard, umbrella, nothing). Posture is the fourth parameter: upright-chest (confident), lean-back (showman), hunched-forward (grouch), tiptoe (eager), slump (tired).

**Variety recipe.** A character is a tuple (class, archetype, hat, neckpiece, prop, posture, accent colour). Classes: tableware and kitchen, tools and office objects, plants and vegetables, foods and sweets, farm and wild animals, vehicles, weather and celestial (sun, moon, cloud, star), instruments, buildings. Archetypes: host, dandy, grouch, sweetheart, tough, dreamer, know-it-all, scamp. Two bots may share at most two tuple entries. That rule alone yields hundreds of distinct bots.

**What reads as the era rather than generic "cute".**
- Pie eyes, not dot-plus-highlight eyes.
- Constant-width tube limbs, not tapered or jointed limbs.
- Gloves and big shoes on everything that has limbs.
- An ink outline of uniform weight around every shape (see §10), no outline-less flat vector.
- Idle bob (§9). A static character is a generic mascot; a bobbing one is a 1930s cartoon.
- Faces on objects: the mug has a face, the pencil has a face, the moon has a face.
- Avoid: pastel gradients, soft drop shadows, glossy highlights, large sparkling irises, chibi proportions, rounded-rectangle "blob" bodies. Each of these is a different decade.

### 9. Animation rules

**Frame rate.** Author at 24 fps, animate on twos (12 drawings per second) for all character motion; drop to ones (24 drawings) only for smears, fast impacts and anything under 6 frames. Export loops as frame sequences or Lottie/sprite sheets with explicit frame holds; never let a renderer interpolate between drawings. UI runs at the device rate; characters run at 24.

**The bob.** Every visible bot idles with a vertical bob of 3 to 5 percent of its height on a 12-frame loop (one bob per beat at 120 BPM) drawn as 6 drawings on twos: down-down-bottom-up-up-top. The bottom of the bob is the downbeat; if music or a UI sound is playing, align the bob's bottom frame to the beat. Secondary parts (hat brim, bow tie tails, cane) lag the body by 2 frames.

**Timing table** (24 fps, drawings on twos unless noted; "f" = frames):

| Action | Anticipation | Main action | Settle | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Blink | 0 | 4f closed (2 drawings) | 0 | 4f, every 3–6 s |
| Nod / shake (per swing) | 2f | 6f | 0 | 8f per swing, 2–3 swings |
| Wave | 4f (arm rises) | 8f per swing, 3 swings | 6f | 34f |
| Hop | 4f squash to 85% height | 10f airborne, stretch to 115% at apex | 4f landing squash, 6f settle | 24f |
| Take (surprise) | 6f hold, body shrinks 10% | pop in 1 drawing on ones (2f) to accent: eyes out, hat up | 10f hold accent, 8f settle | 26f |
| Double take | 2 head shakes of 6f, then 6f anticipation | 2f pop | 10f hold, 8f settle | 38f |
| Point / present | 4f | 6f arm extends with 2f overshoot to 110% | 6f | 16f |
| Walk | — | 12f per step, 24f cycle, 6 drawings per step | — | loop |
| Fast walk / scurry | — | 8f per step, 16f cycle | — | loop |
| Enter (pop in) | 0 | 2f scale 0→115%, 2f 115→95% | 2f →100% | 6f |
| Exit (zip out) | 4f lean back | 2f smear, 2f gone | dust puff 8f | 16f |
| Settle after any move | — | overshoot 2–4f at 108–112% | ease back 4–6f | — |

Squash and stretch: preserve volume; squash to no less than 80 percent height, stretch to no more than 125 percent. Arcs: every limb end travels on an arc, never a straight line. Follow-through: appendages (ears, tails, ties, hat) stop 2 to 4 frames after the body.

**Smears.** Exactly 1 or 2 frames, on ones, only when a limb or whole body crosses more than a quarter of its own height in those frames. Smears are elongated drawings (the 1942 Warner kind), not motion blur. Multiples (three to five copies of a hand along the arc) are allowed for shakes and waves. Never hold a smear; never apply blur filters.

**Loops.** Show loops honestly; a 24 or 48-frame idle that visibly repeats is correct for the era. Do not stitch transitions to hide a loop seam.

**Accessibility.** Honour `prefers-reduced-motion`: replace bob and hops with a single 8-frame settle; no smears. No luminance flicker exceeding 2 percent at any rate; the film "flicker" of §10 is off by default on all surfaces.

### 10. Illustration rules

**Line.** One ink weight per asset class: characters 2.5 px at 1x (10 px at 4x master), props 2 px, backgrounds 0 (painted, no outline). Line is warm near-black ink, not pure black. Lines taper at ends and are slightly irregular; draw or brush them, do not stroke vector paths with a uniform stroke. Allow line boil: if an asset is animated, redraw the outline per frame; if static, provide 2 or 3 alternate line passes and cycle them at 6 fps only in "showcase" contexts, never in dense UI.

**Paint.** Flat fills only inside outlines. No gradients, no inner glows, no cast shadows. Shading, where needed, is a second flat tone on at most 20 percent of the shape's area, hard-edged. Backgrounds are soft wash: watercolour or scanned paper texture with visible paper grain, lower saturation than characters by about 30 percent, so characters pop as cel paint over wash.

**Texture.** Paper grain on cream surfaces at 3 to 6 percent opacity, multiplied. Film grain only on full-bleed illustration or hero panels, never on text or controls, rendered as a half-resolution noise layer refreshed at about 6 Hz with `mix-blend-mode: multiply` (the approach the Cuphead site uses), plus a vignette of no more than 15 percent at corners. Random dust specks: at most 3 on screen at once, each lasting 2 to 4 frames. Hairline "scratches" at most one every 5 seconds. All of this is a theme option ("Reel") that defaults off on iOS and on in marketing surfaces.

**Palettes.** Hex values are our own; they are not sampled from any studio's work.

Contrast: ink on cream is roughly 13:1; ink-soft on cream exceeds 7:1; never place cel-white text on mustard or sky. Red is never the only signal; pair it with an icon or label.

### 11. UI rules

**Hierarchy is a stack of cards.** The 1930s title card and the Cuphead level card share a structure: a flat cream plate, an ink border with a decorative corner, one hand-lettered headline, one line of smaller caption type, and at most one ornament. Map that to UI: page title plate (hand-lettered display, one per screen), section cards (plate with 2 px ink border and 2 px cream-deep inset line), body in a legible sans. Depth is shown by stacking plates with a 2 px offset, not by shadows.

**Frames and ornaments.** Three border styles only: plain ink rule; scalloped "ticket" edge; filmstrip sprocket edge (hero and media only). Corner ornaments are ink-only line drawings (a star, a swirl, a tiny face) at 16 to 24 px, never coloured.

**Type sizes.** Display (hand-lettered or Limelight-class) 32 to 64 px, caps, tracking +2 percent, one per screen. Headline (geometric sans, bold) 20 to 28 px. Body 16 px on web and Mac, 17 pt on iOS, line height 1.45, in a geometric or humanist sans with a double-storey "a" for legibility. Captions 13 to 14 px, never in display faces. Hand lettering is never used below 24 px.

**Buttons.** Pill or rounded-rectangle with 2.5 px ink outline, cream fill, ink label. Primary: poster-red fill, cel-white label, ink outline. Hover: plate lifts 2 px and does one 12-frame bob. Pressed: squash to 94 percent height for 4 frames, then settle. Disabled: mid-grey outline and label, no fill. Minimum target 44 × 44 pt on iOS, 32 px on Mac/web.

**Result and status moments.** Borrow the "card slams in" pattern for completed tasks: a plate pops in (6-frame enter), a hand-lettered word ("Done!", "Sent!", never Cuphead's words) with a 2-frame overshoot, a bot does a take or a bow, then the card settles. Total under 1.2 seconds, dismissible immediately, suppressed under reduced motion.

**Keeping modern UX clarity.**
- Navigation, forms and tables use the ink/cream tokens and the border styles but no character animation. Bots appear in a sidebar, empty states, confirmations and errors, not inside data rows.
- One moving character per viewport at a time. Idle bob is allowed for the active bot only; others hold a drawn pose.
- Icons are single-weight ink line drawings at 1.5 px stroke, 20 or 24 px grid; faces on icons only for bots, not for functional glyphs.
- Dark mode is not "night-time cartoon"; it is ink plates (#1C1917) with cream type and the same accents, grain off.
- Never let the Reel effects (grain, vignette, dust) overlap text, inputs or focus rings. Focus ring is 2 px mustard outside a 2 px ink outline.
- Error copy is plain and specific; the bot's grimace is decoration, not the message.

### 12. Sound rules

**Instrument per moment** (practice derived from cartoon pit-orchestra convention and the Sweet Justice approach of building effects from real objects):

| Moment | Source | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Hover / focus | muted trumpet "wah" or woodblock tick | 60–120 ms |
| Tap / confirm | xylophone single note (C5–G5) | 80 ms, one note per bot pitch |
| Success | xylophone 3-note rise, or cups tapped with sticks | 300–400 ms |
| Error | muted trombone "wah-wah" descending two notes, or slide whistle down | 400 ms, max once per 2 s |
| Send / exit | slide whistle up + short cymbal choke | 300 ms |
| Notification | bicycle bell or glockenspiel ding | 200 ms |
| Loading | ragtime piano vamp loop, 2 bars at 120 BPM | fade in over 500 ms |
| Bot speech | pitched gibberish from a real instrument (kazoo, muted trumpet, clarinet) per bot, never words | 1–2 syllables per text line |
| Big moment | 4-bar big-band stab with brass and drum kit | only for end-of-flow states |

Pitch each bot: assign a base note; its taps, speech and stinger live within an octave of it so a user can identify the bot by ear.

**Processing chain to read as an optical track** (apply as a bus, not per sound):
1. High-pass at 100 Hz, 12 dB/oct.
2. Gentle shelf down from 2 kHz reaching about −12 dB at 8 kHz, low-pass at 10 kHz (a softened Academy curve; the historical roll-off was −20 dB at 8 kHz, which is too dull for UI).
3. Tape or tube saturation, 2–4 percent THD, with 0.2–0.4 percent wow at 0.5–1 Hz.
4. Slow bus compression, 3:1, 30 ms attack, 300 ms release, 2–3 dB gain reduction.
5. Noise bed: pink noise and light crackle at −48 dBFS, ducked 6 dB under speech-like sounds, present only while a sound plays plus a 200 ms tail so silence stays silent.
6. Mono-sum below 300 Hz; narrow stereo width to about 40 percent.
7. Sidechain: when the music bus exceeds −18 dBFS, duck effects 3 dB so they interact the way Cuphead's runtime mastering does.

Music is the lead, effects are the cues: ship fewer effects than you design (Sweet Justice shipped about 30 percent). Loop points must land on bar lines; all stingers end, none fade. Vintage processing is off when the OS is in a reduced or assistive audio mode, and all sounds are mutable independently of music.

| Headline sans | Futura | Jost | enable `ss01` for single-storey a; 400–700 ([Learn UI](https://learnui.design/blog/futura-similar-fonts.html)) |
| Body sans | Kabel, Metro | Josefin Sans (variable, 100–700) | 1920s geometric roots; high x-height; body-capable |
| Heavy rounded display | Cooper Black | Lilita One or Rammetto One | for stickers and "Done!" words |
| Slab | Memphis, Stymie | Arvo or Zilla Slab | tables, numerals |
| Brush script | 1930s show-card brush | Yellowtail, Oleo Script, Kaushan Script | captions on cards, never body |
| Hand lettering | Fleischer title cards | draw it | the wordmark and screen titles are drawn per word; treat them as illustrations |

Rules: no more than three families per surface (one display, one sans, one script). Hand lettering and Limelight are decoration; everything a user must read to complete a task is set in Jost or Josefin Sans. Numerals in tables are tabular. Do not use fan "Cuphead" fonts or files extracted from the game; the public-domain replica Chailce Noggin is legally safe but visually a copy of the Cuphead mark, which fails §7 rule (2).

### 14. Do-not-copy list

Never reproduce, closely paraphrase, or silhouette-match the following. "Closely paraphrase" includes changing colour, species or a prop while keeping the construction.

**Studio MDHR (all protected):** Cuphead and Mugman (a cup or mug head with a bendy straw, liquid "hair", red or blue nose); Ms. Chalice; Elder Kettle; King Dice; The Devil; Porkrind; Chef Saltbaker; every boss named in §6 and their level titles; Inkwell Isle and Inkwell Hell; the Cuphead wordmark and lettering; "A Knockout!", "Ready? Wallop!" and the graded result card layout; the soul-contract and "debtor" framing; the pink parry colour-coding; the Die House; every music track and the barbershop title theme; The Cuphead Show's redesigns.

**Disney:** modern Mickey, Minnie, Pluto, Goofy, Donald, Clarabelle, Horace, the Three Little Pigs and the Big Bad Wolf, the Silly Symphonies and Mickey Mouse title cards and logos. *Steamboat Willie* Mickey is public domain but the name and design are live trademarks; do not use.

**Fleischer and successors:** Betty Boop (any version; the 1930 version is public domain but trademark disputes continue), Bimbo, Koko the Clown (as a specific design), Popeye, Olive Oyl, Bluto, Wimpy (King Features), the Fleischer/Famous title lettering as a wordmark, the bouncing-ball Screen Songs branding.

**Warner / Harman-Ising:** Bosko, Honey, Buddy, Porky Pig, the Looney Tunes and Merrie Melodies concentric-ring title cards.

**Iwerks, Lantz, Van Beuren, Terrytoons, others:** Flip the Frog (public domain, still do not use), Willie Whopper, Oswald the Lucky Rabbit (public domain 1927 design; Disney holds later rights and marks), Woody Woodpecker, Van Beuren's Tom and Jerry and Cubby Bear, Molly Moo-Cow, Farmer Al Falfa, Kiko, Puddy, Felix the Cat (trademarked).

**Constructions to avoid even without a name:** a round black-eared head with two circular ears (Mickey construction); a dog with long droopy ears and a flapper face (1930 Betty); a sailor with forearms wider than his biceps and a pipe (Popeye); a cup-headed hero with a straw (Cuphead).
